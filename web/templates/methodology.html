Methodology

How each metric is computed, what it captures, and what it doesn't.

What this measures (and doesn't)

Every metric here scores the output of a districting process — the map itself — against widely-cited fairness benchmarks. None of them prove intent, and none of them compute Voting Rights Act compliance, which requires Citizen Voting-Age Population disaggregation this tool does not perform. Race columns are not in the precinct dataset and are not joined in.

Numbers without context can mislead. Every metric below has a known failure mode; read those alongside the headline value before drawing conclusions.

Compactness

  • Polsby-Popper: 4πA / P². Range 0–1, higher is more compact. Sensitive to the coastline-paradox effect on perimeter — a district with a jagged but uncontroversial border (rivers, mountains) scores low even when nothing is wrong with it. Always interpret with eyes on the map.
  • Reock: ratio of district area to its smallest bounding circle. Range 0–1; long thin districts score low even if the perimeter is smooth.
  • Schwartzberg: 1 / (P / circumference of equal-area circle). Different scaling of the same idea as Polsby-Popper.
  • Convex hull: ratio of district area to the area of its convex hull. Penalizes concavity (cracking and tendrils) more than perimeter shape.

Rule of thumb: Polsby-Popper above 0.30 is typical, Reock above 0.40 is acceptable, convex-hull above 0.60 is acceptable. None of these are legal thresholds.

Partisan symmetry

  • Efficiency gap (Stephanopoulos & McGhee 2015): (wasted_D − wasted_R) / total votes, where a vote is "wasted" if cast for a losing candidate or in excess of the 50% needed to win. Positive values indicate a pro-R map under the PlanScore convention used here. The 7% threshold is the academic presumptive-gerrymander line; it has no federal legal force after Rucho v. Common Cause (2019). State-court doctrines vary.
  • Mean-median difference: median(D share) − mean(D share). A positive value means the median seat sits above the statewide mean — generally pro-D. Values above ±2–3% are suggestive.
  • Partisan bias: shift every district's two-party share so the statewide mean lands at 50%, then count how many districts the favored party still wins above 50%. Positive values are pro-D.
  • Declination (Warrington 2018): an angle-based asymmetry measure. Undefined when one party wins zero districts — there's no second cluster to compare against. Flagged as null in the JSON contract with a sibling reason field.
  • Lopsided margins: Welch's t-test on D-win margins vs. R-win margins. A significant difference suggests one side's votes are being packed (wasted in lopsided wins) more than the other's.

All of these treat 2020 presidential precinct returns as the reference election. Other cycles would produce different numbers; they are not currently joined in.

Competitiveness

Count of districts whose two-party D share falls in [45%, 55%] and the tighter [47%, 53%] band. Both bands are reported because the wider band is the standard academic competitiveness threshold and the tighter band shows truly close races.

County splits

County boundaries are intersected with district boundaries; fragments below 0.1% of a county's area are treated as slivers and ignored. Output: the count of split counties, the maximum number of districts touching any single county, and the per-county detail.

Some state constitutions explicitly value county integrity, and some do not. Whether a particular split count is unusual depends on the state's geography and rules; comparison to neighbor maps is more informative than the absolute number.

Population deviation

The ideal district size is the state's total relevant population divided by seat count. max |deviation| is the largest fractional gap from ideal across districts. Federal courts strike congressional plans whose maximum deviation exceeds roughly 1% without a strong justification (Karcher v. Daggett, 1983); state legislative plans are presumptively acceptable up to 10% total deviation.

Deviation is only computed when the precinct dataset carries a population column. VEST 2020 omits population for several states (Tennessee included), so the field is reported as not computed rather than zero.

Legal context

Rucho v. Common Cause (2019) held that partisan-gerrymandering claims are nonjusticiable in federal court. Gill v. Whitford (2018) sent the Wisconsin case back on standing without reaching the merits. The metrics on this page are academic and journalistic tools — they describe a map's properties. State courts and state constitutions still vary, and several state high courts have adjudicated partisan-gerrymandering claims under state law since Rucho.

Data sources

  • Precinct returns & population: VEST 2020 on Harvard Dataverse (DOI: 10.7910/DVN/K7760H), columns G20PREDBID, G20PRERTRU, TOTPOP.
  • Counties and VTDs: Census TIGER/Line.
  • Plan source per case: linked from the case study page. State bill PDFs are not redistributed here.
  • Reference for cleaned plans: Redistricting Data Hub posts machine-readable shapefiles within days of enactment.

Precincts are joined to districts by representative-point spatial join, then aggregated. Split VTDs (where the bill assigns blocks within one VTD to multiple districts) are mapped wholly to the dominant district when uploads come in as VTD-equivalency CSVs. This is an approximation; for fine-grained accuracy use a prebuilt district shapefile that respects block-level assignments.
